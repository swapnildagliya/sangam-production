SANGAM

संगम  ·  Confluence

An all-female village. A river goddess. A rejected boy.
A calamity born from heartbreak. A queen whose love cost her everything.

Creative North Star

I

Ritual

Movement is meditative before it is explosive. Repetition, collective breathing, ensemble synchronisation. Stillness carries as much weight as the most complex choreography.

II

Spectacle

Strategic projections, fabric as landscape, lighting transformations. The blue dupatta is not a prop — it is the river. When it is pulled away, the audience watches a world die.

III

Emotional Arc

Every climactic moment must be earned through restraint. Joy is most devastating when someone is missing from it. The queen's sacrifice — her life — means nothing to an audience that has not watched her prison become her love.

Five Key Moments

SCENE 01 · BIRTH

What exists now

The Birth & The Birth of the River

Desert village. Baby is born and rejected. Maha cradles the child alone — she knows what she must do. She weeps. Her tears become the first water this earth has ever held. The blue dupatta unfurls FROM her body. She lays the baby on it. The river carries him away to Sun & Moon, who receive him as guardians. Maha stands empty-handed. The village wakes to flowing water and celebrates — never knowing it was bought with a child.

Creative Direction

Begin in total darkness. Desert. Cracked earth. Collective breath. Ganavya's "Call It Luck If You Want To" rises as the baby passes through the ensemble. The village turns away. Maha is left alone with the child. Silence. Then her tears begin — and On the Nature of Daylight (Max Richter) rises. The blue dupatta unfurls FROM her body as she weeps. She lays the baby on it. The dupatta passes through every villager, carrying the baby across the stage. At the far edge, Sun & Moon enter and lift him — they become his guardians in this exact moment. The river remains. Maha is empty-handed. The village wakes to abundance. They celebrate the water. They do not know what it cost. Only Maha keeps her hand at her heart.

"The village built its entire life on a child they threw away. Every drink of water is the boy they rejected. They drink her grief and call it bounty."
SCENE 11 · REJECTION

What exists now

The Queen's Rejection

The queen sees the boy at the feast and rejects him. Kalbeliya is at full energy. Narration follows.

Creative Direction

Build the Kalbeliya to its absolute peak — the boy is in it, dancing with the village, accepted. Then: the music cuts dead. Not a fade — a cut. In absolute stillness, the Queen makes one gesture. The villagers step back, one by one. The boy reaches toward someone he danced with moments ago — and they look away. This is not one queen's cruelty. This is a room of people failing a cosmic test. He simply understands. The audience should feel the violence of silence — and the violence of being unloved.

"The village does not fail because the queen rejects him. The village fails because no one chooses him."
SCENE 13 · DROUGHT

What exists now

Chaos & Drought

The river disappears. Dead trees. Strobe, smoke machine. Blue dupattas vanish.

Creative Direction

The blue dupattas must be physically taken — violently. Multiple dancers seize them and drag them offstage in a kinetic storm. The audience watches the river die. The ensemble becomes fragmented, searching. The collective breath of Scene 1 is shattered. Uma collapses, unseen.

"The audience must feel the absence of what was always there."
SCENE 21 · SACRIFICE

What exists now

The Queen's Sacrifice — Ardhanarishwar

The Queen dies — but her soul enters the boy. He becomes Ardhanarishwar: half him, half her. Two opposites, one divine whole. Song: TBC. Swapnil & Svetlana.

Creative Direction

Strip everything. No ensemble. Perhaps no music — only breathing or wind. Her movement becomes smaller, lower — then inward. She does not fall. She gives. He reaches toward her; she does not reach back. She places her hand against his chest — and her soul crosses over. The lights shift before any music does. The Queen's body dies; the boy stands up changed — moving with both his weight and hers. Ardhanarishwar. Two became one. The audience understands what has happened before the village does. She is not lost. She chose where to go.

"She does not surrender. She becomes him. Love is the only thing that could undo the drought — and it took two souls in one body to prove it."
S22–25 · BECOMING + RETURN

What exists now

Finally Free, Rainfall, Return & Holi

S22 — Finally Free (Stef Bos): The boy alone with what just happened. First breath as Ardhanarishwar. Solo. Swapnil. S23 — Rainfall: Ghanan Ghanan. S24 — Return: Ghar More Pardesiya. S25 — Holi. Maha returns as River Goddess. Full company.

Creative Direction

S22: The Queen's body is gone. The boy stands alone. Eindelijk Ben Je Vrij — Stef Bos — rises in Dutch. "Finally you are free." He moves with both his weight and hers. The audience watches a body learning to be two. It is the most intimate moment of the show. S23: Silence. Total blackout. One drop. Then another. Then a monsoon. Blue dupattas re-enter one by one. Maha emerges from the centre — the River Goddess returning to her source. S24–25: The boy is received, celebrated, loved. By the time Ghanan Ghanan hits full force, every body is water. The Holi finale is devastatingly joyful — but the queen's place is empty. The village celebrates. The boy celebrates. The person who made it all possible is not there. The audience must feel that absence even as they are swept into the joy.

"Finally you are free — both of you. Joy is most devastating when someone is missing from it."

Cast of SANGAM

Five named characters carrying the dramatic arc of the show.

K

The Queen

Rani Kanō

रानी कनो  ·  Queen of the village

Played by Svetlana Bubnova

Arc

Rigid and fearful → catalyst for catastrophe → redeemed through ultimate sacrifice. She acts from terror, not malice. Control is her religion and her prison — until she dissolves both, offering her life to send her soul into the boy's heart. She does not live to see the world she heals.

Movement Vocabulary

Imperial, grounded, commanding. Early scenes: stillness that demands attention. As she unravels: increasingly desperate, frantic. Scene 21: bare, still — then, in the act of giving, she moves inward. The final gesture is not collapse. It is release. Her last movement is the quietest thing on the stage.

Costume Direction

Royal and structured. Deep indigo, black, burgundy. Heavy jewellery. By the sacrifice scene, layers removed — human fragility beneath.

M

The Boy

Manu / Kavi

मनु / कवि  ·  The poet, the bridge

Played by Swapnil Dagliya

Arc

Hidden, innocent → gradually discovered and accepted → publicly rejected, heartbroken → transformative bridge to the village's healing. Seeks connection, not validation.

Movement Vocabulary

Gentle, fluid, responsive. Adult: quiet longing. Rejection: collapse or stillness — never rage. Appeal (S19): completely exposed, dancing with his entire being.

Costume Direction

Plain, natural materials. Simple clothing as a child. Contemporary, unstructured as an adult. By the appeal scenes — minimal, emphasising vulnerability.

S07 AdulthoodS08 DiscoveryS19 Appeal ◆S21 Sacrifice ◆S22 Finally Free ◆S24 Return
P

The Sun & The Moon

Prabha & Chaaya

प्रभा & छाया  ·  Sun & Moon · Light & Shadow

Played by Shreya Vaidya & Laurien De Ridder

Arc

Cosmic caretakers who protect the boy not through ownership but through unconditional presence. They do not own him — they shelter him. Their role is freedom, not possession.

Movement Vocabulary

Flowing, aerial, celestial. Veils suggest light and shadow, day and night. Near-synchronisation — two cosmic bodies orbiting the same centre. Rooted in Bollyfolk but with an ethereal, otherworldly quality.

Costume Direction

Flowing fabric suggesting wings. Light colours — ivory, pale gold, soft grey. Movement should do the work, not the costume.

V

The Messenger

Vayu

वायु  ·  The Wind

Played by Narcisse Merlier

Arc

Neutral observer and active messenger between worlds. Carries the village's plea to the Four Tribes. Neither judge nor saviour — the medium through which cosmic forces communicate.

Movement Vocabulary

Fluid, directional, purposeful. The wind doesn't hesitate. Movement has clear intention and direction. Unlike Sun & Moon (ethereal) or the queen (grounded), Vayu is always in transit.

Costume Direction

Contemporary and neutral — distinct from the village aesthetic. Could suggest both masculinity and androgyny. The wind has no fixed form.

S14 Tribe InvitationS19 Appeal
U

The River Goddess / Mother

Uma

उमा  ·  The source of all water

Played by Maha

Arc

Mother of two children — the boy and the river. Both born the same moment, from the same grief. She creates the river to send her rejected baby downstream to Sun & Moon, who become his guardians. The village drinks her tears and calls it abundance — they never know. When the village rejects her child a second time (S11), her heart breaks again and the river dies — there is no more baby to send away, the water has nowhere to go. The drought is not punishment. It is love withdrawn. The rain returns only when the boy is finally loved (S21).

Movement Vocabulary

Grounded, maternal, fluid as water. S01: stillness, then weeping — and the dupatta unfurls from her body. She lays the baby on the river she just made. She does not collapse. She gives. Through Part 1: she carries the secret. Her hand stays at her heart while the village celebrates the abundance she paid for. Drought (S13): she simply stops flowing — no theatre, no scream. The river withdraws because she has nothing left. Finale (S23–24): she returns not with drama but with inevitability. Water always finds its level.

Costume Direction

Blue silks and water-like fabrics throughout. In the final revelation — deep river blue with gold. She is the blue dupatta; when it is taken away, so is she.

Where SANGAM Comes From

The original concept, choreography inspirations, and visual references that shaped the production.

Origin Concept — April 2026

PURNA

पूर्ण  ·  Whole, Complete  ·  The concept that became SANGAM

In a distant land, there exists a village in the desert — a place where no men have ever been born, where women coexist with cracked earth, dust, and sky. They know no water. They have never known any.

One day, a woman becomes pregnant. When her child is born, the village gasps — it's a boy. The village turns away. The mother — Maha — is left alone with her rejected child. She weeps. And as she weeps, the first water this earth has ever held appears at her feet. A river is born from her grief, not for the village, but for her baby — so he can be carried somewhere he will be loved.

The rejected baby travels downstream on the river his mother just made for him. At the far edge of the village, two celestial beings — the Sun and the Moon — receive him. They become his guardians in that exact moment because Maha trusted them with what she could not keep. They raise him with the wisdom of light and shadow, teaching him the dance of opposites: feminine & masculine. From them he learns the only thing worth learning: to love and be loved in return.

Years pass. The boy returns. The village discovers him, hesitates, then accepts him — for a moment. At the feast, the Queen sees him and rejects him publicly. The village steps back. The boy reaches out to someone he just danced with. They look away. This is the second rejection — and Maha cannot bear it. Her heart breaks again. The river withdraws. The drought begins. There is no more child to send away; the water has nowhere to go.

The village panics. They call on Nataraja, they summon the Four Tribes — ritual, strength, community, prayer. Each tribe gives everything. None of it is enough. None of it is love.

Only then does the Queen understand. She does not surrender — she gives. She places her hand on the boy's chest and her soul crosses into him. Her body dies. He rises moving with both his weight and hers — Ardhanarishwar. Two souls in one body. Two opposites made whole.

Now the boy has been loved twice — once by Sun & Moon, once by the Queen. The river returns. The rain falls. The village embraces him — not as disruption, but as the embodiment of wholeness — of Purna, the eternal circle of love, sacrifice, and unity. But the Queen's place is empty. And only Maha knows what it took.

7 Works That Shape the Production

Each reference informs a specific scene, texture, or movement quality in SANGAM.

Beginning Sequence

ganavya — ami pana so'dras

The opening ritual quality. Tamil devotional music meeting contemporary minimalism — intimacy, stillness, the sacred before it becomes spectacle. Directly informs Scene 01's collective breath and the pre-verbal atmosphere before any movement begins.

LUMINI
A Contemporary Dance Piece

Choreography Inspiration

Lumini (A Contemporary Dance Piece)

Light as character. How darkness and emergence work together — bodies appearing from nothing, disappearing into nothing. Directly relevant to Scene 13 (drought blackout) and Scene 22 (the rainfall's first drop in total dark).

Aayirathil Oruvan
Celebration Of Life

Choreography Inspiration

Aayirathil Oruvan — Celebration Of Life

The tribal energy, scale, and visceral joy of the Holi finale. How Indian folk choreography commands a large stage with collective rhythm and colour. Direct reference for Scenes 17–18 (Bhangra & Bollyfolk tribes) and S25. The joy that exists even in the queen's absence.

Stages of Grief
AVANTGARDE SHOW 2023

Choreography Inspiration

Stages of Grief — AVANTGARDE SHOW 2023

Collective suffering staged physically — how grief moves through an ensemble as fragmentation, isolation, searching. Reference for Scene 13 (the ensemble breaks apart as the river dies) and Scene 19 (the village's appeal, searching together for something lost).

The Final Stage
Girar O Mundo

Choreography Inspiration

The Final Stage — Girar O Mundo

The language of sacrifice and release. What a body looks like when it gives itself completely — not collapse, but dissolution. Core reference for Scene 21. The queen does not fall. She moves inward. Her last gesture is the quietest thing on the stage.

Many Angels
Lar Lubovitch

Choreography Inspiration

Many Angels by Lar Lubovitch

The original Purna vision for Prabha & Chaaya: the Sun and Moon as guardians. Aerial, flowing duet work that expresses unconditional presence — sheltering without possessing. Direct inspiration for Scenes 02–03 and the Sun & Moon movement vocabulary throughout.

Max Richter
The Four Seasons

Choreography Inspiration

Max Richter — The Four Seasons (Dance Video)

Structural approach to the production arc — how seasonal and cyclical change maps onto bodies. Classical structure carrying contemporary movement. Relevant to the overall rhythm of 24 scenes and the idea of nature as the dominant character driving the human story.
